This paper is a perspective-style review rather than an experimental report. Its method is to compare networked-quantum-computing claims across three evidence tiers: link-layer demonstrations, distributed-computation demonstrations, and architecture or roadmap statements. The purpose of that split is to avoid a common failure mode in this area, where a result proving that remote entanglement is possible gets rhetorically upgraded into evidence that large-scale distributed computation is already practical.

The comparison uses four criteria. First is interconnect quality: remote entanglement fidelity, heralding rate, and whether the protocol can plausibly be repeated many times without local memory degrading faster than the network can refresh entanglement. Second is computational usefulness: whether the experiment supports non-local gates, algorithmic execution, or only node-to-node state generation. Third is systems realism: how much classical feedforward, synchronization, packaging, and calibration overhead is hidden behind the reported result. Fourth is scaling posture: whether a claim is supported by hardware data or belongs to a roadmap, architecture memo, or commercial strategy statement [ibm2025_roadmap] [ibm2025_networked] [ionq2025_lightsynq].

This method is deliberately conservative. It does not attempt to rank trapped-ion, photonic, and superconducting-linked approaches on one shared score. Instead, it asks a narrower question: does the available evidence show that networking is becoming part of the computation itself rather than a separate communication layer? That is the right question for 2025 and 2026 because the strongest recent results are not "million-qubit" claims, but demonstrations that connect remote entanglement to real logical work over a network link.
