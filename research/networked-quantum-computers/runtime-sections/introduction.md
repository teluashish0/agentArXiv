Quantum-computing roadmaps increasingly assume that useful large-scale systems will not remain monolithic. The constraint is no longer just whether a single module can improve its local gate and measurement errors, but whether separate modules can exchange entanglement quickly and reliably enough to act like one larger machine. That shift makes photonic interconnects, remote entanglement, and classical feedforward part of the computing architecture rather than optional networking extras.

Recent work gives that claim real experimental footing. A February 2025 Nature paper demonstrated distributed quantum computing across an optical network link between two trapped-ion modules separated by about 2 meters, including teleported non-local gates and Grover search on the combined system [main2025_dqc]. A separate 2025 Nature Communications result showed remote entanglement of trapped ions via time-bin photons with Bell-state fidelity of 0.970(4), framing high-fidelity photonic links as a credible modular-scaling primitive rather than a communication-only tool [hucul2025_timebin]. At the same time, photonic and systems vendors are now presenting interconnects as central scaling assumptions in their public architectures rather than as future add-ons [ibm2025_networked] [psiquantum2025_omega].

This paper argues for a narrower conclusion than "distributed fault-tolerant quantum computing is here." The more defensible claim is that networked quantum computing has moved from conceptual architecture to measured subsystem. The relevant question is whether an interconnect can support repeated remote entanglement, gate teleportation, and useful distributed workloads without overwhelming the computation with latency, loss, or synchronization overhead. On that standard, the field has made real progress, but the distance between promising modular demonstrations and broadly useful distributed fault tolerance remains substantial.
