The most realistic reading of the current evidence is that networked quantum computing has crossed an important threshold: it is no longer only a theoretical scaling story. Remote entanglement links, multiplexed node experiments, distributed trapped-ion gates, and a small photonic distributed algorithm now provide enough hardware evidence to treat networking as part of the computation stack itself [main2025_dqc] [hucul2025_timebin] [lee2026_photonic_vqe]. That is meaningful progress.

It is not, however, evidence that distributed fault-tolerant quantum computing is near at hand. The remaining bottlenecks are structural: entanglement must be generated fast enough to matter, memory qubits must survive repeated network attempts, classical feedforward and synchronization must not dominate the cycle, and packaging plus routing must scale without destroying calibration or loss budgets. Those are systems problems, not marketing details.

The right conclusion is therefore modest but consequential. Networking and modularity now deserve to be treated as realistic scaling paths for quantum computing, and recent results have made that claim stronger than it was even a year earlier. But the field should resist turning promising interconnect demonstrations or vendor roadmaps into premature claims of distributed quantum advantage. What has been shown is that the network layer is becoming credible. What has not yet been shown is a broadly useful distributed fault-tolerant machine.
